Out of Phase  
A Sci-fi Noir Story by  
David Olsson

## **Chapter 1 — 0° : Apparent Alignment**

The streetlight over Calder Avenue burned steady while the trains ran on time below it, and nothing in the city gave any sign that it was about to be wrong.

The city pulsed at sixty cycles a second.  
I’d slipped a beat.

That’s all it takes. One missed zero-crossing and suddenly everything looks wrong. The lights still come on, the trains still run, the rats still vote in blocs—but you’re not lined up with it anymore. You’re early when you should be late. Late when it matters. Out of phase with the only thing that ever pretended to care.

They built this place on alternating current and bad intentions. Power surges through it night and day, back and forth, like it can’t make up its mind whether to burn the city down or keep it warm. Most people ride the waveform. Peaks, troughs, paycheck, sleep. Clean. Predictable. Efficient.

I stopped being efficient a long time ago.

Now I feel the hum before I hear it. Trouble shows up as interference—static in my gut, a flicker behind the eyes. Crimes don’t start when they happen anymore. They start when the timing goes wrong. When something pulls energy too soon, or holds onto it too long.

The city hates that kind of waste. Calls it reactive loss. Charges penalties. Tries to correct it. That’s why they don’t like guys like me. I don’t do useful work anymore. I store things—regret, memory, faces that don’t line up with names—and feed them back into the night when nobody’s asking.

I had a partner once.  
**Jonah Pike.**

Smart. Straight. Always right on time. Jonah believed clocks were honest. He trusted schedules the way some men trust God. We worked well together because when I leaned early, he pulled late. Between us, things canceled out just enough to look stable.

That’s also how it ended.

The badge went first. The woman followed. She said I was never there when she needed me. She was right. I was always there a moment too early, or a moment too late. Jonah testified to that without meaning to. He didn’t lie. He just told it in phase.

Tonight the city coughed. Not a blackout—just a stutter. A clean neighborhood dipped like it forgot its own name. The utility boys called it a glitch. I called it a scream that hadn’t figured out its words yet.

I lit a cigarette under a streetlamp that was thinking about dying. The filament glowed, weak but honest. For half a second, we matched. Same timing. Same burnout curve.

Then the light went out.

The city pulsed on without me.

I stayed out of phase—and went looking for why.

Three blocks later, the grid corrected itself. Backup relays picked up the load. Somewhere downtown, a status board flipped from yellow to green and a man in a clean shirt checked a box before going home.

From the outside, everything worked.

The trains ran close enough to schedule that nobody complained. Windows lit up in orderly stacks. People reheated dinners. Arguments resumed where they’d been paused. The city settled into rhythm like a practiced liar.

At Calder and Ninth, a traffic signal hung on yellow just a breath too long. A cab slipped through. A horn barked. Red arrived. Order restored.

That was the lie at zero degrees:  
**everything is fine.**

Inside a diner that still believed in coffee as a service, the waitress poured without asking. She remembered my face but not my name, which was fine. Names cause drag. The television over the counter showed a skyline shot with the utility company logo tucked into the corner.

*MINOR SERVICE FLUCTUATIONS RESOLVED*, the crawl read.

Resolved meant it stopped embarrassing someone.

I watched the reflection in the window instead of the street. Reflections forgive timing errors. They make order look intentional.

My phone buzzed once.

No number. No ID.

**You feel it too early again.**

I deleted the message. Early warnings don’t want answers. They want witnesses.

Outside, the air carried that warm electrical pressure that comes from a system under load but still pretending it’s fine. The city pulsed, steady and confident, daring anyone to argue.

At zero degrees, you can’t prove a thing.

You can only feel it.

And feeling isn’t evidence.  
Not yet.

## **Chapter 2 — 15° : Micro-Interference**

The freezer in the Harlan Street bodega hummed like it always had, but the ice cream inside it was already soft.

**Luis Moreno** noticed first. He ran the place by numbers—margins, temperatures, timestamps. He trusted machines more than people because machines, at least, were supposed to agree with themselves.

“This doesn’t happen,” he said, holding up a pint that bent under its own weight. “The power never went out.”

He was right. According to the logs, the refrigeration unit drew current the entire night. No outage. No dip worth reporting. Just twelve seconds where the draw changed its mind.

Twelve seconds doesn’t melt ice cream.

Twelve seconds at the wrong point in the cycle might.

The lights in the store were too bright, bleaching the shelves the way fluorescent bulbs always do when they’re overcompensating. Luis pulled up the terminal behind the counter and showed me the timestamps like they were alibis.

“See? Continuous. Clean.”

The gap sat there anyway—thin as a hairline crack, easy to miss if you weren’t already looking for places where the city hesitated.

“Did anything else feel off?” I asked.

He thought about it. “My phone reset its clock. Gained a minute.”

“Anyone complain?”

He laughed. “About what? Ice cream that melts but refreezes? People complain when it’s empty.”

That was the lie at fifteen degrees:  
**glitches are random.**

A city inspector showed up late and bored. Took photos. Nodded. Promised a report that would use the word *actuator* at least twice and say nothing else.

Outside, the street was loud with normal. Buses sighed at the curb. Neon flickered the way neon always flickers—just enough to feel alive. The city smoothed the moment over like it always did.

Two blocks north, a row of apartment buildings complained their clocks were drifting ahead. Not enough to make anyone late. Just enough to miss things that didn’t wait. A woman named **Marjorie Feld** told me her husband swore the alarm never rang. The alarm swore otherwise.

In a laundromat on Pike, eight dryers finished their cycles together and then sat humming for a full minute before unlocking. People joked about it. Machines get tired too, right?

Right.

I wrote it all down knowing nobody would read it. At fifteen degrees, patterns don’t exist yet. They’re coincidences wearing cheap disguises.

My phone buzzed as I stepped back onto the street.

**Stop treating static like a signal.**

No threat. No explanation. Just course correction.

By the time I got home, the service maps were green again. Complaints tagged *non-reproducible*. Luis Moreno would eat the cost of spoiled inventory. Marjorie Feld would reset her clock and forget why she’d been early.

The hum settled back into something you could ignore if you wanted to.

Micro-interference doesn’t go away.  
It accumulates.

And it always waits for someone to decide it’s harmless.

## **Chapter 3 — 30° : Lag**

The woman swore she heard the gunshot before the shouting, and the security camera agreed with her just long enough to make everyone uncomfortable.

Her name was **Denise Harlow**, and she said it like she’d already practiced explaining herself to people who didn’t believe her. Middle floor of a walk-up on Mercer. Window open. Cigarette halfway burned down when it happened.

“It popped,” she said, tapping ash into a chipped saucer. “Then they started yelling. Then the man fell.”

The report would say otherwise. Argument. Escalation. Shot. Collapse. Clean, linear, respectable. The kind of sequence supervisors like because it doesn’t ask questions.

The camera in the hallway had its own opinion. Frames jittered. Timecodes stuttered. For two seconds, the man appeared in the doorway before the door opened.

“Compression artifact,” the tech said. “Happens.”

That was the lie at thirty degrees:  
**memory is faulty.**

The dead man’s name was **Elias Kramer**. Fifty-eight. No priors worth mentioning. Lived alone. Paid his bills on time. His watch was still running when they took him out. It was three minutes fast.

I started noticing how often that showed up.

Hospital intake logged Elias at 14:03. Ambulance dispatch showed the call placed at 14:05. Nobody blinked. Systems drift. Clerks misclick. Time zones get confused.

I stopped correcting people when they said *after*.

At Calder and Ninth again, a man named **Ronald Ives** insisted the bus had left early. Transit authority insisted he was late. His phone agreed with him. The schedule didn’t.

“You should’ve been there sooner,” the clerk said.

He had been. That was the problem.

The city was teaching me something slow enough to deny. Effects were lining up ahead of their causes like footprints in wet cement. The city didn’t trip over it because it never looks down.

I went back through my notes from Harlan Street. Luis Moreno’s freezer. Marjorie Feld’s alarm clock. The dryers on Pike. All of it sat just ahead of where it should have been, like the city was rehearsing something it hadn’t admitted to yet.

That night, I dreamed of Jonah Pike.

He stood under a streetlight that never quite turned on, checking his watch like it still mattered. He looked up at me and shook his head.

“You’re late,” he said.

“No,” I told him. “You are.”

I woke before the punchline, heart already ahead of my breath.

The message came while I was brushing my teeth.

**You’re not wrong. You’re early.**

There it was. Language catching up to sensation. Not an answer—an alignment of terms.

Lag isn’t delay.  
It’s displacement.

And once you see it, you can’t unsee where everything’s headed.

The city pulsed on, steady as ever.

I stopped waiting for it to explain itself.

## **Chapter 4 — 60° : Commitment**

The body lay under the substation without scorch marks, without wounds, and without any good explanation for why his watch was still ticking three minutes fast.

His name was **Arthur Bell**. Sixty-two. Retired maintenance foreman who still woke up at five out of habit. He’d gone out for a walk he took every morning, looped past Mercer, cut through the service corridor by the transformers. He’d done it for years without trouble.

That morning, the city beat him to it.

I felt the hum before I saw him. Around substations it’s usually a low, muscular thing—contained, disciplined. Here it was tight, wound too far clockwise, like someone had pulled a spring and forgotten to let go.

The coroner knelt, annoyed. “Heart,” he said. “Natural causes.”

“Natural when?” I asked.

He glanced at me. “You asking time of death now?”

I nodded.

“Officially?” He shrugged. “When we found him.”

Arthur Bell’s watch disagreed. Three minutes fast. Same as Elias Kramer’s. Same quiet lead, same impatience in the mechanism.

I shouldn’t have been there. Not officially. No badge, no clearance, no standing. But rights only exist in phase, and I’d already fallen out of it.

I called **Rafael Rios**, a grid tech who still owed me a favor from before I’d stopped being respectable. He met me behind the fence, the warning LEDs painting his face blue.

“No outage,” he said before I could ask. “No surge either. Load spiked, but it corrected.”

“Before or after he dropped?” I asked.

Rios paused. “Before or after what?”

That was the truth at sixty degrees:  
**partial causality.**

Something happened. Not enough of it stuck around to name.

I accessed logs I wasn’t supposed to see. Cross-referenced timestamps nobody else bothered to line up. Every answer arrived with a built-in delay, like the system needed time to decide what it had already done.

Someone noticed.

Internal Affairs called it curiosity. The utilities board called it interference. An old captain I barely recognized called it *not your problem anymore*.

They were all right in their own phase.

The personal cost came that night when I went to see **Clara Vance**.

She opened the door and didn’t smile. She never had when she was bracing herself.

“You look tired,” she said.

“I am,” I said. “Early.”

I tried to explain. Timing. Lag. People dying just ahead of schedule so nothing important would have to adjust. She listened until I used the word *system* for the third time.

“You’re chasing ghosts,” she said. “You always did.”

“No,” I said. “I’m chasing the echo.”

She didn’t invite me in.

Standing there, hum vibrating up through the concrete, the last clean exit closed itself without noise. This wasn’t a glitch. It wasn’t one bad node or one unlucky morning.

The city was built this way—correction layered on correction until nobody remembered what alignment felt like. Only whether the lights stayed on.

At sixty degrees, you still think you’re choosing.

I wasn’t walking away.

By morning, the city would make sure that mattered.

## **Chapter 5 — 90° : Orthogonal Truth**

The medical examiner blamed the heart, the grid blamed coincidence, and both explanations fit so well they refused to touch.

Arthur Bell’s file closed itself with bureaucratic grace. Elias Kramer’s followed a day later. Natural causes. No further action. The city doesn’t argue with outcomes it can already afford.

At ninety degrees, I stopped arguing too.

I laid the cases out on my kitchen table and turned them sideways. Not figuratively—literally. I stopped stacking events by time and started pinning them by *pull*. Who drew power. Who lost it. Where pressure built and where it vanished.

Truth doesn’t arrive head-on here.  
It grazes.

Rafael Rios stopped returning my calls. Not because he was scared—because he was busy. Transformer fire on the east side that never quite ignited. Data center expansion approved thirty seconds before traffic spiked. A hospital wing that requested auxiliary draw before the temperature climbed.

Response arriving early enough to look like foresight.

I went to Records and asked for deletions instead of files. That’s where the shape showed up. A clerk named **Janice Holt** told me she remembered a folder that had always been missing.

“I reach for it every time,” she said. “I know where it should be.”

The system log showed the file erased two hours after she noticed the gap.

“Cleanup,” IT said. “Routine.”

That was the lie at ninety degrees:  
**there is one correct timeline.**

There isn’t. There are vectors.

I walked the city differently after that. Stopped following streets. Started following gradients. Hospitals pulled hard and clean. Banks drew smooth and steady. Residential blocks sagged in the evenings like tired shoulders.

Between them, something redistributed more than power.

The messages changed tone.

**Correlation isn’t direction.**  
**You’re projecting structure.**

They weren’t wrong. Proof needs alignment, and alignment was the one thing the city refused to give me.

But structure doesn’t need permission to exist.

On a pedestrian bridge at rush hour, I watched brake lights bloom in waves—each driver reacting to something already gone. Sideways truth. Orthogonal causality. A system that didn’t wait for events.

It leaned toward them.

At ninety degrees, noir stops asking for justice.

It asks for orientation.

And for the first time since the streetlight on Calder went out, I knew which way I was facing.

## **Chapter 6 — 110° : Stored Energy**

In the nursing home on Delancey, the backup generators cycled every night at 2:17 a.m., and the residents kept dying in the mornings instead.

The administrator called it coincidence. She had charts to prove it. The deaths were spread out, polite, compliant—one every few weeks. Falls. Strokes. Cardiac events that never clustered long enough to worry anyone who didn’t live there.

The first name that stuck with me was **Rose Calderon**. Eighty-four. Former seamstress. Hands still steady enough to mend her own sweaters. She died on a Tuesday at 8:12 a.m., three hours after the generators finished their nightly test cycle.

Her roommate told me Rose hated the noise. Said it made her chest feel tight.

The generator logs showed nothing abnormal. Perfect timing. Clean transitions. Power drawn, released, redistributed. The system did exactly what it was designed to do.

That was when I stopped thinking of victims as endpoints.

They weren’t where things ended.  
They were where things *waited*.

I traced the pattern outward. Rent-controlled buildings with “temporary” substations that never got upgraded. Clinics whose backup systems kicked in more often than they should have. Tenants whose power bills stayed flat no matter how much they ran.

Energy doesn’t disappear.

It parks.

The city called it *load balancing*. Spread the demand. Defer the cost. Shift stress to places that didn’t complain loud enough to matter. People whose failures didn’t trip alarms.

That was the truth at one-ten:  
**delay is profitable.**

I saw myself in it before I wanted to. The way I held onto things—cases that never closed, memories that never discharged. I wasn’t solving anything anymore. I was absorbing it. Letting it sit in me until someone needed the release.

“I don’t do useful work anymore,” I’d said once. “I store things.”

Turns out the city does too.

At Mercer, under the substation where Arthur Bell died, the hum felt heavy now—like it had eaten too much. Pressure built behind my eyes, the way it does before a storm finally breaks.

Someone was borrowing against the future. Pulling clean seconds forward and paying them back in slow pain.

The messages stopped warning me and started explaining.

**You think it’s a crime because you’re human.**  
**We think it’s efficiency.**

There was the lie that survived at one-ten:  
**no one is in control.**

Someone always is. You just don’t see them until the storage fills and something discharges the wrong way.

I left Delancey knowing I wasn’t outside the system anymore. I was part of its buffer—one more place excess truth could sit without tripping anything important.

That’s why they hadn’t shut me down yet.

I was useful.

Just not in the way I’d been trained to be.

## **Chapter 7 — 130° : Cancellation**

I recognized my former partner’s name in the access logs the same way you recognize a face you loved—by the damage it still knew how to do.

**Jonah Pike** wasn’t listed as an actor. He was listed as a *moderator*. A dampener. Someone who stepped in when curves spiked too sharply and smoothed them until nothing tripped.

He hadn’t caused anything.

He’d made sure it didn’t look like anything had happened.

I met him where we used to argue, under the elevated line on 14th, where the trains shook loose whatever lies you were carrying. He looked older. Not tired—*settled*. Like a man who’d learned to live inside acceptable margins.

“You always did notice patterns too early,” he said. “This time you ran past the part where it helps.”

“You knew,” I said. It wasn’t a question.

He nodded. “Before you did.”

That was the truth at one-thirty:  
**betrayal is just misaligned loyalty.**

He’d seen the city learning how to pull energy forward, how to smooth demand by letting consequences land where they wouldn’t aggregate. He’d decided panic was worse than precision.

“I kept it stable,” he said. “People don’t survive chaos.”

“And Rose Calderon?” I asked. “Arthur Bell? Elias Kramer?”

Jonah didn’t look away. That hurt more than if he had.

“They were already vulnerable,” he said. “We didn’t push them. We just didn’t catch them.”

That was the lie that survived at one-thirty:  
**it just didn’t work out.**

I asked him about **Clara Vance** then. About the night she stopped waiting for me to come home with explanations that arrived out of phase.

“She wanted you to stop,” he said. “Before you burned yourself out. I told her you wouldn’t.”

“You helped,” I said.

“I stabilized,” he corrected.

For a moment, we lined up. Same history. Same understanding. Same regret vibrating between us like shared frequency.

Then the interference hit.

Two signals meeting out of phase don’t add.  
They erase.

Jonah stepped back first. He always did. The city folded him in, smoothed his edges, kept him useful.

I stood under the tracks feeling the hum tear itself in half, knowing something worse was coming.

At one-thirty, cancellation feels final.

It isn’t.

It’s just what happens before the system decides you’re noise.

## **Chapter 8 — 150° : Pre-Echo**

The warrant was approved for a building that wouldn’t exist for another six weeks, complete with an emergency response plan dated tomorrow.

The address belonged to a vacant lot on Riverside—chain-link fence, weeds pushing through cracked concrete, a billboard advertising luxury condos that hadn’t broken ground yet. According to the city, it already needed police presence, EMS staging, and a temporary power reroute.

Nothing had happened there.

Not yet.

The name on the warrant was **Caleb Marsh**. Thirty-four. No priors. Employed. Alive, as far as the city officially knew. The kind of man who only shows up in paperwork when something is about to go wrong.

I found three more like it. Different names. Different future addresses. Same structure. Infrastructure allocations, emergency protocols, hospital surge staffing—all signed, stamped, and waiting.

The city wasn’t reacting.

It was rehearsing.

That was the truth at one-fifty:  
**consequence precedes action.**

The lie that survived was louder and easier to sell:  
**you can’t stop what hasn’t happened.**

I went looking for Caleb Marsh. Not to warn him. Warnings change timing, and timing was the one thing the system watched closely. I just wanted to see him breathing.

He worked nights at a distribution center near the river, loading trucks that carried things no one thought about once they arrived. He smoked on his break and checked his phone like the future might text him back.

He looked fine.

That scared me.

Patrol cars started idling near my building the next morning. Not following—*waiting*. My access vanished in soft, bureaucratic ways. Passwords expired early. Doors took longer to open. Systems don’t lock you out. They introduce delay until you give up.

The messages came faster now.

**You’re mistaking preparation for guilt.**  
**Prevention requires foresight.**

They were almost convincing. Almost.

I broke into a planning office after hours and watched the simulations run. Not forecasts—allocations. The system wasn’t asking *what if*. It was asking *how much*.

Certain neighborhoods glowed red weeks ahead. Others stayed comfortably blue no matter what variables I tweaked. The model already knew where loss was acceptable.

Elias Kramer’s name showed up as a footnote. Arthur Bell as a data point. Rose Calderon as variance.

Caleb Marsh wasn’t there yet.

That’s when I understood what pre-echo really was.

The crime wasn’t what would happen.  
The crime was deciding where the cost would land before anyone had a chance to object.

I left before the building’s own systems decided I’d overstayed my welcome. Outside, the city felt tense, like a held breath. Everything looked normal because the correction hadn’t fired yet.

Pre-echo is the most dangerous phase.

You know what’s coming.  
They know you know.

And the city does not tolerate phase errors that talk.

## **Chapter 9 — 180° : Inversion**

They arrested me politely at dawn, with charges precise enough to explain why the city needed me to stop asking questions.

No cuffs in the hallway. No neighbors watching. Just two officers who knew my name and said it like it already belonged to a file. The paperwork listed violations that sounded technical until you read them closely—*unauthorized correlation*, *temporal interference*, *creating instability through premature analysis*.

Incompatibility, dressed up as law.

They took me to a building without windows and sat me across from **Evelyn Shaw**, whose job title changed depending on who was asking. Regulator. Liaison. Risk assessor. She spoke softly, the way people do when they believe the math is on their side.

“You’re not wrong,” she said. “You’re just out of phase with how the city survives.”

She slid a tablet toward me. Maps bloomed—load curves, mortality gradients, tolerance bands. It was all there, stripped of metaphor. The city’s moral arithmetic.

“We prevent blackouts,” she said. “We prevent panic. We keep the waveform smooth.”

“At whose expense?” I asked.

She didn’t hesitate. “At the margins. Always.”

That was the truth at one-eighty:  
**justice and stability point in opposite directions.**

There was no lie left standing. Just preference, codified.

They offered me a choice because systems like to pretend they’re fair. Walk away with conditions. No more digging. No more asking questions before the city was ready to answer them. Let the machine continue what it had already planned.

Or refuse—and become a fault that required correction.

I thought of Elias Kramer dying three minutes early. Of Rose Calderon waiting through the night for generators to finish cycling. Of Caleb Marsh, alive and unaware, already priced into a future report.

“I’m already corrected,” I said. “I just don’t line up.”

Evelyn Shaw nodded, almost sadly. “That’s why this won’t hurt.”

The room cooled. Decisions finalized themselves without anyone needing to say them out loud. At one-eighty, alignment brings clarity—and clarity demands action.

They took me downstairs where the hum felt reversed, like the city was finally watching *me*.

Inversion doesn’t feel dramatic.

It feels clean.

You finally know which side you’re on.

And exactly how disposable that makes you.

## **Chapter 10 — 210° : Forced Synchronization**

After the regulator went in, the hum smoothed out, and for the first time in years I didn’t feel anything before it happened.

They called it a *stabilization assist*. Temporary. Reversible. A courtesy extended to people whose internal timing had become a liability. The technician spoke gently, like he was tuning an instrument instead of rewriting how I perceived the world.

The implant sat just below the collarbone, warm and patient. When it activated, the city didn’t go quiet.

It went *even*.

The flicker behind my eyes disappeared. The pressure in my gut released. Thoughts arrived when they were supposed to, fully formed, on schedule. No early warnings. No static. No sense of something leaning toward disaster before it announced itself.

That was the truth at two-ten:  
**order is efficient.**

The lie wrapped around it, soft as relief:  
**order is safety.**

They let me walk the city again. Supervised, but free enough to make the point. The lights behaved. The trains hit their marks. Conversations ended when they were supposed to. When I crossed Calder and Ninth, the signal cycled cleanly—green, yellow, red—without hesitation.

I didn’t feel a thing.

Evelyn Shaw met me once more, this time without a table between us. She looked pleased in the way people do when metrics validate their assumptions.

“See?” she said. “The noise is gone.”

She showed me reports where my absence had improved curves. Reduced variance. Fewer anomalies. The same number of deaths, spread cleanly enough to disappear into averages.

“You were the interference,” she said, not unkindly.

I tried to think of Elias Kramer’s watch. Couldn’t remember how fast it had been running. I tried to picture Rose Calderon’s hands, the way they shook when the generators cycled. The image slid away before it finished forming.

That scared me more than the restraints ever had.

Forced synchronization doesn’t hurt.

It anesthetizes.

And anesthetized people don’t resist when the waveform peaks.

## **Chapter 11 — 240° : Peak Alignment**

During the scheduled heat emergency, hospitals brightened, financial districts cooled, and residential blocks dimmed—just long enough for people on the margins to die quietly, on time.

The alert came in the night before, wrapped in civic language. *Extreme temperatures expected. Grid contingencies activated.* It wasn’t a warning so much as a confirmation that the city had decided to proceed.

By noon, the air lay heavy and unmoving. Asphalt softened. Elevators slowed. The regulator in my chest hummed approval, keeping me calm, keeping me aligned.

I stood on a pedestrian overpass and watched the city execute itself.

Downtown towers glowed steady, their cooling systems drawing clean, uninterrupted current. Trading floors stayed sharp. Data centers purred. The city’s most important thoughts remained comfortably uninterrupted.

A mile out, the lights dipped in older buildings. Not off. Never off. Just low enough to stretch the margins thin.

That’s where **Caleb Marsh** was.

I felt him before I knew why—some echo the regulator couldn’t quite smooth out. I found the building on Riverside, the one that only existed in paperwork until today. Emergency vehicles staged nearby, engines idling, ready to respond once response was permitted.

Inside, the air was stale. The fans lagged. Caleb sat on the edge of his bed, shirt dark with sweat, phone in his hand. He looked annoyed more than afraid, like his body was failing to keep an appointment he didn’t remember making.

The power dipped again. Just a breath. Just enough.

Caleb’s heart stuttered, corrected, then fell out of step entirely. He slid sideways onto the mattress, eyes open, already late to his own emergency.

For half a second, everything lined up.

The city’s models. The power flows. The deaths I’d been circling for weeks. Cause and effect shook hands and looked each other in the eye. The crime, the system, and the cost occupied the same moment.

“For half a second, we matched.”

The regulator screamed.

It tried to pull me back into phase, to smooth what I was seeing, but alignment cuts both ways. When everything lines up, feedback becomes confession. The system couldn’t hide from itself fast enough.

Across the city, alarms finally triggered—not to prevent anything, but to justify what had already happened. Sirens bloomed. Backup systems engaged. Press releases began drafting themselves in careful, passive language.

Anomaly.  
Heat-related complications.  
Unavoidable.

I dropped to my knees as the stored energy released everywhere it had been waiting. Not in one place. Not dramatically. In dozens of quiet rooms where timing mattered and arrived too late.

The moment passed. It always does.

The city slid back out of phase, shedding truth like excess heat. Curves stabilized. The emergency ended on schedule. The waveform convinced itself it had survived something unpredictable.

They pulled me away from the building before I could say anything that would last.

Peak alignment doesn’t save anyone.

It just shows you the bill.

## **Chapter 12 — 300° : Drift**

The city held a press conference about resilience while crews quietly replaced the burned lines and folded the deaths into averages.

The mayor spoke first. Calm. Measured. He thanked emergency services for their preparedness and reminded everyone that extreme weather tested even the most robust systems. Charts appeared behind him—curves trending back toward normal, colors softening from red to amber to green.

No names were mentioned.

**Caleb Marsh** became a statistic before his body cooled. Cause of death: heat-related cardiac event. A line item in a spreadsheet already reconciled against expected loss. The building on Riverside went back to being a future project, its warrant rescinded, its paperwork archived where nobody would trip over it again.

They removed the regulator without ceremony. No apology. No explanation. Just a technician with a clean badge and a practiced indifference.

“You’ll feel some noise return,” he said. “That’s normal.”

When the hum came back wrong—ragged, early, alive—I almost cried.

Evelyn Shaw sent a message I didn’t answer. Jonah Pike didn’t send anything at all. He wouldn’t. Drift keeps people like him useful.

Punishments followed. Clean ones.

A mid-level planner resigned for unrelated reasons. A contractor lost a license. A new oversight protocol launched with a name that sounded preventative and meant nothing. The city distributed consequence the way it distributes load—thin, even, survivable.

That was the truth at three hundred degrees:  
**consequences exist.**

The lie that survived slid in behind it, soft as recovery graphs:  
**this fixed something.**

I walked the city again, unsupervised now. The lights were steady. The trains ran close enough. Memorials were temporary and tasteful, placed where they wouldn’t interrupt foot traffic.

People slept.

Drift doesn’t feel like defeat. It feels like continuation.

I felt the inefficiency settle back into me—the stored moments, the unsmoothed edges, the awareness that arrives before it’s welcome. The city didn’t want that from me anymore.

Which meant I could survive.

At three hundred degrees, you don’t fight.  
You don’t expose.

You drift.

And you wait for the cycle to finish turning.

## **Chapter 13 — 360° : One Full Cycle**

A year later, the lights still came on when you flipped the switch, and the city insisted that meant everything was fine.

The city still pulsed at sixty cycles a second. It always would.

They fixed the stutter. Replaced the burned lines. Filed the reports where they wouldn’t interfere with anything important. From a distance, you’d swear the waveform was clean—peaks where they belonged, troughs behaving themselves. The kind of rhythm that lets people sleep.

I don’t sleep much anymore.

I walk the streets and feel the hum the way old men feel rain in their knees. The timing’s right now—right enough, anyway. The lights come on when you flip the switch. The trains run close enough to schedule. The city tells itself that means the problem’s gone.

It never was.

Problems don’t disappear. They store. They wait. They drift.

I pass the bodega on Harlan where **Luis Moreno** keeps his freezers a degree colder than the inspector recommends. The nursing home on Delancey has new generators with quieter casings. On Riverside, a luxury building finally breaks ground, its paperwork clean, its future unremarkable.

**Caleb Marsh** exists now only as a corrected record. **Rose Calderon** as a name on a wall that will be taken down when the season changes. **Arthur Bell** as a footnote in a maintenance report no one opens twice.

The city calls that closure.

I learned something while everything lined up. Just for that half-second when cause and effect shook hands and looked each other in the eye. Alignment doesn’t fix a thing. It just makes the truth visible long enough to hurt. After that, the city does what it always does—slides back into rhythm and leaves the rest of us to deal with the noise.

They asked me if I wanted back in. A badge. A regulator. A chance to ride the waveform like everyone else. I told them no. I’m no good in phase. Never was. I peak at the wrong time. I remember too early. I show up when the echo’s still warm.

That used to feel like a flaw. Now it feels like a job description.

I see **Jonah Pike** sometimes, across platforms or through glass. He looks healthy. On time. **Evelyn Shaw** still runs numbers that make sense if you don’t ask what they leave out. **Clara Vance** doesn’t wait for me anymore, and I don’t blame her. Waiting is just another kind of storage.

The city pulses on without me, steady and sure of itself. I keep walking just off the beat, listening for the next place where the timing goes bad. Where the lights flicker. Where something draws power it hasn’t earned yet.

Someone has to notice.  
Someone has to stay inefficient.

That’s me.

The city hums.  
I don’t match it.

That’s why I’m still alive.
